How Bain's sample cases work

Bain's sample cases show a "good answer" and a "better answer" for key steps. The better answer adds the pros and cons of each idea, the risks, and the next steps. That's the habit to build.

Good vs. better

The difference interviewers notice

Ten terms to drop naturally

Revenue drivers

Revenue equals customers times what each one spends. Spend is price times items, times how often they buy.

Key purchase criteria

What customers care about most when they choose where to buy, like style, price, convenience, or easy returns.

Brick-and-mortar

Physical stores, as opposed to online shopping.

Omnichannel

Letting customers shop across stores, websites, and apps as one experience, like buying online and returning in store.

Price elasticity

How much demand changes when price changes. If a 10 percent price rise cuts sales by 20 percent, demand is elastic.

Promotion

A temporary discount to bring in shoppers, like a seasonal sale.

Rewards program

Members get ongoing perks, like a standing discount, in exchange for joining and coming back.

Activation fee

A one-time fee to join a program. In this case, we assume members pay it, so it counts as revenue.

Pull-forward

When a sale just moves purchases people would have made anyway into the sale window, instead of creating new ones.

What would have to be true

A test of your answer: what assumption would need to change for the other option to win?

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is FashionCo, a women's fashion company with a long history in the industry. Its revenue has declined every year for the past five years.

The CEO has a management meeting at the end of the week and wants our recommendation. Two questions: what's causing the decline, and what can FashionCo do to grow revenue?

Your move

Restate both questions in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So FashionCo's revenue has fallen five years in a row, and before Friday's meeting the CEO wants to know what's driving the decline and how to grow revenue again. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes." There are two questions here. Keep both in mind: a great answer to the second one won't save you if you never answer the first.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with practice answers

  • Is the whole market shrinking, or just FashionCo?
    Good question. We have some market data to show you shortly.
  • Where does FashionCo sell: stores, online, or both?
    Mostly through its own stores. Online is a small share.
  • Who is the typical FashionCo customer?
    Professional women, mostly over 40. The brand is known for classic workwear.
  • Is the goal revenue this year, or long-term growth?
    Both, but the CEO needs something that moves revenue this year.

These answers were written for practice. Bain's case leaves the details to the interviewer.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Take a minute or two to plan your approach. How would you structure this?

Your move

Bain suggests asking yourself three things: what could drive falling revenue, what customers care about when they buy, and what numbers and evidence could solve this. Sketch your buckets, then walk through them.

Strong framework

Why is FashionCo's revenue falling, and how can it grow again?
1Market trends
  • Is women's fashion growing?
  • Shift from stores to online
  • New competitors and technology
2Customers
  • What styles they want now
  • What they care about when buying
  • Is FashionCo's base aging?
3FashionCo's revenue
  • Number of customers
  • Spend per customer: price × items
  • By channel: stores vs. online
4Ways to grow
  • Change styles or channels
  • Change price
  • Drive traffic: sales, ads, rewards

Say it like this

"First, I'd check whether this is a market problem or a FashionCo problem: is women's fashion growing, and how are shopping habits changing, like the shift online? Second, customers: what styles they want and what they care about when they buy. Third, FashionCo's own revenue, split into number of customers and spend per customer, by stores and online. Then I'd look at ways to grow. My hypothesis is that the market is fine but FashionCo hasn't kept up with how women shop now, so I'd like to start with market and consumer trends."

Why it works

  • It separates a market problem from a company problem, which answers "why" quickly.
  • It uses Bain's own prompts: drivers, purchase criteria, and evidence.
  • It sets up the second question, how to grow, so the case flows.

05

Analyze market and consumer trends

Interviewer

Good place to start. Here's some data on the market and on FashionCo. What stands out?

Which finding should you lead with?

The market grew about 11 percent while FashionCo fell from 1.25 billion dollars to 1 billion. So it's a FashionCo problem. Online went from 25 to 40 percent of the market, but only 12 percent of FashionCo's sales. Casual and social wear grew from 30 to 45 percent of the market. And FashionCo's average customer is getting older, from 46 to 50, so it isn't winning new, younger shoppers.

Then

Answer the first question, what's causing the decline, in three sentences. Use Bain's hypotheses as a check.

Say it like this

"The decline is a FashionCo problem, not a market problem: the market grew about 11 percent while FashionCo fell 20 percent. Shoppers are moving online and toward casual and social wear, and FashionCo is behind on both. Its customers are also getting older, which tells me it isn't attracting the next generation of shoppers."

Bain's case suggests similar hypotheses to raise: more sales moving online, competitors adding technology in stores, loyalty apps with mobile payment, and shoppers wanting easier returns and more online options.

06

Brainstorm ways to grow revenue

Interviewer

What could FashionCo do to grow revenue?

Your move

List ideas out loud for 60 seconds. Then, for each one, say a pro, a con, and how practical it is.

Good answer: lists different ways to grow

  • Shift styles toward social wear to match what shoppers want now.
  • Raise prices to earn more on each item.
  • Increase volume with sales and advertising that bring more shoppers into stores.

Better answer: adds pros, cons, and practicality

Shift styles

Pro: could attract more and younger customers. Con: could clash with the brand and push away loyal customers.

Raise prices

Pro: if volume holds, revenue rises. Con: volume rarely holds. FashionCo would need to research price sensitivity, and sales would likely fall.

Sales and advertising

Pro: no change to styles or pricing strategy. Con: the extra revenue has to be more than the cost of the marketing.

Grow online

Pro: that's where the market is moving. Con: takes time and investment to build a strong site and easy returns.

Then

Score each idea, then say which you'd pursue this year and which are longer-term bets.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "For this year, sales and advertising can move revenue fastest without changing the brand. For the longer term, the biggest prize is online, where the market is heading, plus a loyalty app with easy mobile payment. Raising prices is easy to do but risky while customers are already leaving."

    07

    Compare the two options

    The CEO will need to approve a new sales and advertising strategy, so the CFO and CMO have narrowed it to two options. The question: which brings in more revenue this year?

    Option A: rewards program

    From Bain's case

    • 10 million customers, flat.
    • 25 percent join in year one.
    • Average spend before any discount: 100 dollars a person a year.
    • One-time activation fee: 50 dollars.
    • Members always get 20 percent off.

    Option B: intermittent sales

    From Bain's case

    • 10 million customers, flat.
    • Average spend before any discount: 100 dollars a person a year, spread evenly over the year.
    • 20 percent off for 3 months a year.
    • During those 3 months, purchases double.
    Your move

    Start with today's revenue as the baseline. Then work out each option, one customer group at a time. Say your assumption about who pays the activation fee.

    1. 1 · Baseline10M customers × $100$1,000M
    2. 2 · A: members2.5M × $80 = $200M, + 2.5M × $50 fees = $125M$325M
    3. 3 · A: total7.5M × $100 = $750M, + $325M$1,075M
    4. 4 · B: per customer$75 for 9 months + ($25 × 2 × 80%) = $75 + $40$115
    5. 5 · B: total10M × $115$1,150M
    6. 6 · Compare$1,150M − $1,075MB by $75M

    A note on the gap: Bain's page says Option B wins by 50 million dollars. Its worked calculation is shown as an image, which isn't in the page text. Using the assumptions exactly as written, and counting the activation fee as revenue, the gap is 75 million. Either way, Option B wins, and the interviewer cares more about your approach than the last digit.

    Quick check: what share of customers would need to join the rewards program for Option A to match Option B this year?

    Each member adds 50 dollars in fees but gives up 20 dollars in discounts, a net 30 dollars. To add 150 million, matching Option B, you'd need 5 million members, which is half of all customers. That's the "what would have to be true" test: twice the expected sign-ups.

    Then

    Summarize the math in two sentences, and add one "what would have to be true."

    Say it like this

    "Option B brings in about 1.15 billion this year, versus about 1.075 billion for Option A, so B wins by about 75 million. For A to catch up this year, half of all customers would have to join, twice the estimate. And in year two A gets weaker, because the activation fees stop but the 20 percent discount keeps going, unless members start spending a lot more."

    Year two, worked out: with no new fees, Option A drops to about 950 million, because 2.5 million members still get 20 dollars off. To match Option B, members would need to spend about 200 dollars a year, double what they spend today.

    08

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The CEO just walked in. What do you recommend?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Answer both of the CEO's questions, then give risks and next steps.

    Good answer

    "We recommend Option B, intermittent sales, because it brings in more revenue this year than the rewards program."

    Better answer

    "Revenue is falling because FashionCo hasn't kept up with shoppers moving online and toward casual and social wear. For this year, we recommend Option B, intermittent sales, which brings in about 75 million dollars more than the rewards program."

    "Two risks. The rewards program could win long term if more customers join in year two and members spend more. And sales alone won't fix the real problem: if FashionCo doesn't change its styles and build up online, the decline will continue."

    "Next steps: test whether rewards members would spend more over time, and come back to the CEO with a plan for styles and online, backed by a customer survey."

    The better answer is what Bain's case points to: the same pick, plus risks and next steps, and it answers both of the CEO's questions, not just the second one.

    Bonus drills: sizing and promotions

    Two short reps in case the interviewer adds a question.

    Market sizing: dresses sold in the U.S.

    Interviewer

    Estimate how many women's dresses are sold in the U.S. in a year, and what they're worth.

    Your move

    Go top-down. Start with women, narrow to buyers, then multiply by how many they buy and the price. Say each assumption out loud.

    1. Start broadAdult women in the U.S.≈130M
    2. × 60% buy a dress each yearDress buyers≈80M
    3. × 3 dresses eachDresses a year≈240M
    4. × $50 average price240M × $50≈$12B
    5. Sanity checkA big brand with a few hundred million in dresses ≈ a few % share✓
    6. Biggest uncertaintyAverage price: $30 → ≈$7B; $80 → ≈$19B✓

    All inputs are interview-style assumptions, not market research.

    Promotions: does a sale really add revenue?

    Interviewer

    Our math assumed purchases double during the sale. What could make that assumption wrong?

    Your move

    List two or three reasons, then say how you'd test the assumption.

    Strong answer

    Pull-forward

    Some shoppers just wait for the sale and buy less the rest of the year, so the "doubling" partly steals from other months.

    Training customers

    Regular sales teach people never to pay full price, which can hurt the brand over time.

    Margin, not just revenue

    Revenue rises, but 20 percent off on everything in the sale months lowers profit per item.

    How to test

    Run the sale in some stores and not others, then compare full-year spend, not just sale-month spend.

    Quick math

    If purchases only rise 50 percent during the sale instead of doubling, Option B's sale months bring 25 times 1.5 times 80 percent, or 30 dollars per customer. Total spend is 105 dollars, or 1.05 billion, now below Option A's 1.075 billion. The answer flips, which is why this assumption needs testing.
